After Johannes Kepler, 1619

Harmonices Mundi

Kepler’s claim

In the fifth book of Harmonices Mundi (1619), Johannes Kepler asked what the planets would sound like if we could hear their motion. He took each planet’s angular speed as seen from the Sun, slowest at aphelion and fastest at perihelion, and read the ratio between the two as a musical interval. Each planet, he said, sings a slide that rises and falls once per orbit.

The width of that slide depends only on how stretched the orbit is: fastest ÷ slowest is ((1 + e) ÷ (1 − e))², where e is the eccentricity. Modern values land close to what he heard.

Earth’s semitone is the famous one. Kepler spelled it mi, fa, mi, and heard in the syllables miseria and fames: misery and famine, which he said hold sway on our planet.

What you’re hearing

Sing is Kepler’s method: each planet hums at a pitch proportional to its angular speed. In Kepler’s six the distances are compressed so all six fit on screen, and chosen so their average notes form an A major chord. The eccentricities are real, so the width of each slide is his. The score gives each planet its own lane, stretched so you can see the shape: a brief peak at perihelion and a long low stretch at aphelion.

Pluck plays a note each time a planet crosses a needle. Closer to the star is higher. For a circular orbit the note is proportional to how often the planet goes around, so orbits that resonate also harmonize: 2:1 is an octave, 3:2 a fifth, 4:3 a fourth.

The other skies

TRAPPIST-1 is a red dwarf about 40 light-years away with seven roughly Earth-sized planets. Neighboring periods sit near 8:5, 5:3, 3:2, 3:2, 4:3 and 3:2, which is why the system plays a chord rather than noise. Periods and eccentricities are real; starting positions are illustrative.

Jupiter’s moons. Io, Europa and Ganymede are held in a 1:2:4 Laplace resonance, which keeps all three from ever lining up at once. Callisto sits outside the lock.

Planets here feel only their star, not each other, so every closed orbit repeats forever. Tempo stretches time without changing pitch.
